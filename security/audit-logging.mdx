Every Cardinal-evaluated transaction generates a set of structured evidence — a risk decision, supporting findings, and a unique request identifier. Capturing that evidence in a durable, tamper-evident audit log is what transforms Cardinal from a runtime control into an accountable, auditable part of your transaction infrastructure.

## Why Audit Logging Matters

Without a complete audit record, you cannot answer the questions that matter most after an incident or during a compliance review:

- Was this transaction checked before it was signed?
- What did Cardinal return, and what did your system do with that result?
- Did the user acknowledge a `REVIEW` warning before proceeding?
- Does the on-chain outcome match the evaluated intent?

A well-structured log answers all of these questions and gives you the evidence to investigate anomalies, satisfy regulators, and demonstrate that your controls functioned correctly.

## What to Log for Every Transaction Check

Capture the following fields for every Cardinal API call, regardless of the decision returned.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Cardinal Fields">
    These fields come directly from the Cardinal API response:

    | Field | Description |
    |---|---|
    | `canonical_intent_hash` | SHA-256 hash of the serialised canonical intent you submitted |
    | `cardinal_request_id` | The unique `request_id` returned by Cardinal — use this for reconciliation |
    | `recommended_action` | The Cardinal decision: `ALLOW`, `REVIEW`, or `BLOCK` |
    | `risk_score` | Numeric risk score (0–100) returned by Cardinal |
    | `risk_level` | Human-readable risk band: `LOW`, `MEDIUM`, or `HIGH` |
    | `findings` | Array of risk findings returned by Cardinal; may be empty for `ALLOW` |
    | `checked_at` | ISO 8601 timestamp of when the Cardinal check completed |
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Your System Fields">
    These fields are generated by your own application and policy layer:

    | Field | Description |
    |---|---|
    | `policy_result` | What your system decided to do: `ALLOW`, `REVIEW`, `BLOCK`, or `QUEUED` |
    | `user_ack` | For `REVIEW` decisions, the timestamp of the user's explicit acknowledgment; `null` otherwise |
    | `settlement_route` | The routing path chosen for the transaction (e.g. `direct`, `safesend`, `escrow`) |
    | `tx_hash` | The on-chain transaction hash after broadcast; set after settlement |
    | `initiator_ref` | Your internal reference for the user or service that initiated the transaction |
    | `approver_ref` | Reference for the approver, if a second sign-off was required |
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## What Not to Log

Your audit log must never contain credentials or key material. Storing these fields alongside transaction records creates a high-value target and may violate your security policy, contractual obligations, or applicable law.

<Warning>
Do **not** log any of the following:
- Cardinal API keys or tokens
- Wallet private keys or mnemonic seed phrases
- Raw signing keys or hardware security module secrets
- Passwords, session tokens, or OAuth refresh tokens
- Identity documents or regulated personal data beyond what your compliance framework requires
</Warning>

If an error message contains a credential value, redact it before writing to the log. Build redaction into your logging pipeline, not as an afterthought.

## The Transaction Passport

Think of the complete audit record for a single transaction as a **Transaction Passport** — a self-contained document that ties the Cardinal decision to the on-chain outcome through a chain of verifiable hashes and timestamps.

A Transaction Passport contains:

1. The hash of the canonical intent that was checked
2. The Cardinal `request_id` and full decision payload
3. Your policy result and the reasoning behind any override
4. The user acknowledgment record for `REVIEW` decisions
5. The settlement route and any routing parameters
6. The on-chain transaction hash after broadcast

This structure means you can reconstruct the full decision history for any transaction from a single record, without relying on external systems or Cardinal's own logs.

### Sample Audit Record

```json
{
  "intent_hash": "sha256:abc123...",
  "cardinal_request_id": "req_7f3a9b2c",
  "recommended_action": "ALLOW",
  "risk_score": 18,
  "risk_level": "LOW",
  "findings": [],
  "checked_at": "2026-08-02T10:15:33.000Z",
  "policy_result": "ALLOW",
  "user_ack": null,
  "settlement_route": "direct",
  "tx_hash": "0x1a2b3c..."
}
```

<Note>
Use the `cardinal_request_id` field to reconcile your internal transaction records with Cardinal's own records. If you contact Cardinal support about a specific transaction, this identifier is the fastest way to locate the corresponding evaluation.
</Note>

## Evidence Retention

Retain each Transaction Passport for the **life of the transaction plus a compliance-appropriate period**. The right retention window depends on your jurisdiction, applicable regulations, and the nature of the assets involved — consult your legal and compliance team to define it.

<Tip>
For most blockchain transactions, a minimum retention period of five to seven years is a reasonable starting point, but regulated activities (payments, securities, escrow) may require longer. Define your policy in writing and apply it consistently.
</Tip>

At a minimum, your retention and protection controls should include:

- **Immutability.** Write logs to append-only storage. Audit records must not be modified or deleted during the retention period.
- **Access control.** Restrict write access to the logging pipeline and read access to authorised roles only.
- **Clock synchronisation.** Keep system clocks synchronised so that timestamps across your logs, Cardinal's records, and on-chain data are consistent and comparable.
- **Backup and recovery.** Store logs in at least one secondary location so that an infrastructure failure does not destroy your audit trail.

## Reconciliation

At regular intervals, reconcile your Transaction Passport records against on-chain data to verify that every evaluated transaction settled as expected.

A complete reconciliation checks that:

- Every transaction with a `policy_result` of `ALLOW` has a corresponding `tx_hash`
- Every `BLOCK` decision has no corresponding on-chain activity
- Every `REVIEW` decision has a non-null `user_ack` timestamp before the `tx_hash`
- The `intent_hash` in each record matches a recomputed hash of the stored intent parameters

Flag any discrepancies for investigation immediately. A mismatch between a Cardinal decision and an on-chain outcome is a potential indicator of tampering, a bug in your enforcement logic, or an unauthorised signing event.
